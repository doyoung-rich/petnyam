Heavy Metals in Cat Food: What Headlines Do—and Don’t—Tell You

A worrying headline can make a familiar bag of cat food look different overnight. PetNyam compared the study organization’s public summary with FDA resources to separate reported findings from decisions an individual owner can actually make. Detection is not, by itself, a verdict that your cat’s food is dangerous. This article does not rank brands or claim that PetNyam tested food.

A public owner reaction—not our own feeding experience

A public r/cats discussion about the investigation included owners expressing frustration. That reaction helps explain why a practical checklist is useful; it is not laboratory evidence, a confirmed illness report or PetNyam’s firsthand experience. We did not use commenters’ medical or brand claims as evidence.

What the study organization actually says

The Clean Label Project summary describes testing 100 products from 62 brands for more than 122 contaminants, using an independent ISO 17025-accredited laboratory. It reports lead detection in 99% of the sampled products. Importantly, it explicitly says detection does not establish danger on its own and that category comparisons are not safety determinations for individual products. Read the organization’s summary and linked report.

Our review here is of the public summary, not an independent audit of its raw measurements or a veterinary review of an individual cat. The sample cannot be treated as every food on the market. A headline percentage cannot identify the exposure associated with the particular product, batch and amount your cat eats.

Four questions to ask before accepting a claim

  1. Which products and batches were sampled? A category headline alone does not identify your bag.
  2. What was measured, and in which units? Keep the concentration and comparison basis with the number; do not compare unlike units.
  3. What conclusion does the source support? Detection, category comparison and an individual-product safety assessment are different claims.
  4. What is missing? If the public summary does not answer your product-specific question, mark it as unknown rather than filling the gap with a brand ranking.

A study report is not a recall notice

For U.S. products, check FDA animal-product recalls and withdrawals and the manufacturer’s current notices. Match the product name, package size and lot information to any notice before deciding it covers your purchase. Follow a applicable recall’s instructions; this article does not establish whether any particular batch is recalled.

No matching notice is not a guarantee that a product has zero contaminants. Equally, a general investigation should not be presented as proof that your batch has been recalled. These are separate checks.

Keep a useful product record

  • Photograph the front and back of the package.
  • Record the lot code, best-before date and purchase details.
  • Write down the food, treats and supplements currently fed.
  • Save the original study link and the exact claim that concerns you.
  • Ask the manufacturer whether it can provide relevant product-specific testing information; record what it supplies and what remains unanswered.

These steps are PetNyam’s information-organizing checklist, not a test for heavy-metal exposure. They turn a vague worry into questions a manufacturer or veterinarian can address.

Nutrition still matters when considering an alternative

FDA explains that the nutritional adequacy statement identifies whether a U.S. pet food is intended as a complete diet and for which life stage. Treats and supplements generally are not sole diets. Do not replace an appropriate complete diet with treats or an improvised recipe just because its marketing sounds cleaner. FDA label guidance.

If your cat is on a therapeutic diet, ask the treating veterinarian before changing it. If your cat seems ill, seek veterinary advice rather than trying to diagnose contamination from a headline. This site cannot determine whether a symptom is caused by food.

Quick answers

Does detected mean unsafe?

Not by itself. The study summary expressly distinguishes detection from an individual safety determination. Do not turn its category findings into a diagnosis.

Can this article recommend the safest brand?

No. We have not independently tested products or established a comparable, current dataset for that claim. Affiliate links and customer ratings would not fill that evidence gap.

What should I do first?

Identify the exact product and lot, check current recall notices, and bring your records and specific concern to your veterinarian. Avoid treating fear-driven marketing as a substitute for evidence.
